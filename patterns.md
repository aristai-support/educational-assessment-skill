# Patterns

Executable techniques. Concepts live in the glossary; decision rules live in the cheatsheet.

## Staged Process Portfolio
**When to use**: any high-weight deliverable a generator could produce in one shot (Ch 1, 2, 4, 6).
**How**: split into proposal → outline → draft → peer response → revision → reflection; grade each stage as its own artifact, marking development not polish. Keep the same sequence across every assignment so students internalise the system, not the topic. Require an editable document so revision history is visible.
**Trade-offs**: multiplies marking events; late joiners have no trajectory; a capability discontinuity is evidence for a conversation, never a verdict.

## Extended Resources Disclosure Appendix
**When to use**: on every graded submission, all term — not once (Ch 1).
**How**: replace the bare Works Cited with an appendix listing every resource used — AI tools, writing centre, friends, tutors — answering four questions each: what it was, why they used it, *how* specifically, and anything else you should know. Link prompts and outputs; weight it enough to matter.
**Trade-offs**: relies on honesty, so it is norm-setting, not control. Bolted on as an afterthought it yields nothing usable.

## Critique-the-Output Opening Assignment
**When to use**: first assignment of the term, to establish shared AI vocabulary from evidence rather than assertion (Ch 1).
**How**: students prompt a tool on a self-chosen topic, record prompt and output verbatim, then critique on three axes against assigned readings — accessibility, accuracy, style/tone. The deliverable embeds prompt and output and cites the tool.
**Trade-offs**: fails if you assign summarising a set reading — tools do that well and the critique has nothing to bite on.

## Annotate-the-AI-Draft
**When to use**: the essay is now forgeable and your subject expertise is the scarce resource (Ch 6).
**How**: instruct students to generate the essay, paste it in, then use tracked changes to insert quotations and data from this week's readings that support or challenge each claim. Grade the annotation layer: evidence selection, claim mapping, detection of the model's characteristic failures. Extend into a workshop where groups synthesise annotations into a shared outline.
**Trade-offs**: drafting fundamentals go unassessed — pair with a competency check.

## Oral Defence Add-On
**When to use**: attach to every high-weight assessment; the single highest-leverage change (Ch 2, Ch 11).
**How**: the student presents two or three candidate solutions and justifies the chosen one, live. Structured rubric, sessions recorded for moderation. In quantitative subjects ask how they know their answer is right.
**Trade-offs**: expensive at scale, stressful, exposed to examiner bias — rubric and recording control that, they do not remove it.

## Skill Deconstruction Before Replacement
**When to use**: before retiring any assignment (Ch 6).
**How**: enumerate why you assigned it. For a humanities essay: ideate, formulate a thesis, create sub-claims, arrange them cohesively (not stacked), draft paragraphs with topic sentences, connect evidence to claims. Extend with Bloom's action verbs, then design the replacement against the list, not the format.
**Trade-offs**: slow, and it usually reveals the replacement must be several instruments rather than one.

## Higher-Order MCQ Construction
**When to use**: you need a proctorable instrument but still want critical thinking (Ch 6).
**How**: build from Aiken's five types (premise-to-consequence, multiple premises, classification, dual propositions, relationship-between-concepts) and Haladyna's formats. Default to context-dependent item sets: one stimulus, then a series of items.
**Trade-offs**: slow to author, and proctoring reintroduces the modality equity problem between online and in-person cohorts.

## 4×4 Integration × Literacy Grid
**When to use**: as an assignment generator whenever AI is part of the task (Ch 4).
**How**: cross the four Balanced Integration tenets with the four literacy competencies (Application, Authenticity, Accountability, Agency). Sixteen cells, each yielding a concrete requirement — fact-checking falls out of Accountability, tool comparison out of Application.
**Trade-offs**: generates more requirements than one assignment can carry; select, do not implement all sixteen.

## Tutored Interaction with Alternating Phases
**When to use**: replacing a one-shot submission (thesis, proposal, hypothesis) with an assessable dialogue (Ch 4).
**How**: instruct the AI to tutor, not write — questions, hints, surfacing misconceptions, high expectations. Alternate: independent baseline → AI-assisted exploration → collaborative development where the tutor offers candidates but does not write → independent reflection. Grade the interaction log, the portfolio of modifications, and an evolution document justifying why the final choice diverged from the machine's.
**Trade-offs**: only works if the tutor instructions hold; students will push for the answer, and the divergence narrative can be fabricated unless anchored to the log.

## Bloom's Question–Feedback–Reflection Loop
**When to use**: a learner-facing AI activity that must build thinking rather than substitute for it (Ch 10).
**How**: set a genuine, unresolved, *situated* problem. (1) The learner authors a laddered question set spanning Bloom's six levels. (2) They analyse and interpret the answers, checking for incompleteness, bias, unstated assumptions. (3) They write follow-ups targeting where the first pass was thin. Loop until adequate. Grade the ladder's coverage and ascent, the critique quality, and the follow-ups.
**Trade-offs**: depends on prompt-writing skill, which must be taught first, and on the problem being local enough that the application weakness bites.

## AI-Augmented Inquiry Loop
**When to use**: designing an AI-supported inquiry unit (Ch 7).
**How**: student poses a hypothesis → investigates → AI responds *generatively* with alternative explanations, counter-hypotheses, unconsidered variables rather than a score → student revises → next AI prompt is generated from the revision. The system logs the trajectory as the assessment record.
**Trade-offs**: genuine inquiry runs longer than standardised-testing pacing allows; truncated, it degrades into a quiz. Needs teachers trained to facilitate without answering.

## Four-Part Fairness Protocol for AI-Generated Items
**When to use**: before any AI-generated item reaches a student (Ch 7).
**How**: diverse training data; mandatory human review of every item against the learning objective; multifaceted scoring (AI items *plus* peer and teacher assessment); transparency about why those items were selected and how they map to targeted skills.
**Trade-offs**: human review cancels most of the throughput gain that motivated generation.

## Hattie Feedback Completeness Audit
**When to use**: on any feedback your system produces, human or machine (Ch 8).
**How**: check each message answers all three questions — Feed Up (explicit goal), Feed Back (specific, naming proficiency and gaps), Feed Forward (concrete next steps). Machine feedback defaults to the middle one only. Add the three effectiveness conditions: specificity, immediacy, framing the learner receives as growth.
**Trade-offs**: cheap, catches the commonest failure, but checks feedback form rather than accuracy.

## Run-It-Through-The-Model Item Authoring
**When to use**: the default authoring step for every remaining take-home item (Ch 11).
**How**: submit the item to the model before assigning it. The response tells you exactly what the item now measures. Modify, or drop it.
**Trade-offs**: a snapshot of one model on one day; re-audit each cycle because the weak edge moves.

## Two-Axis Shift (Task Type and Modality)
**When to use**: any quantitative subject where the answer is checkable and the procedure standard (Ch 11).
**How**: *Task type* — drop items the model answers directly; require shown and explained work, reasoning across multiple representations and their relationships, and transfer to a new context. *Modality* — move weight to in-class formative work, proctored tests, and verbal explanation of how the learner knows they are right.
**Trade-offs**: the modality half reintroduces timed-assessment inequity and is unavailable to online cohorts. Do not let it collapse into handwritten timed essays.

## Embed AI, Grade the Refinement
**When to use**: a take-home task that cannot be protected, whose target skill AI can scaffold but not perform (Ch 11, Ch 6).
**How**: require AI use, then require the student to use it to refine *their own* explanation for a specified audience (e.g. a young learner). The graded object is the refinement, not the solution.
**Trade-offs**: assumes the student had an explanation to refine — pair with an independent baseline phase.

## AI Mass-Production of Differentiated Items
**When to use**: you need context-rich, culturally relevant items at a volume manual authoring cannot sustain (Ch 11, Ch 5).
**How**: prompt for real-life-context problems, then re-prompt adding grade level, ability level, student interests, and cultural relevance as constraints. Iterate until fit for purpose.
**Trade-offs**: every item still needs human review; the model is weakest on domain-specific representations and precise technical vocabulary.

## Explicit Use-Boundary Clause
**When to use**: on the assignment sheet of every assessment, before work starts (Ch 2, Ch 5, Ch 10).
**How**: state which specific tasks permit AI and which require independent work, with worked examples of acceptable and unacceptable use for *this* assignment. A prohibition plus a citation rule is not a policy — define authorship.
**Trade-offs**: high-yield and cheap, because ambiguity drives most misuse — but it only holds if the task design, not trust, enforces the boundary.

## Accessibility by Design
**When to use**: at design time for any AI-mediated assessment, never as later accommodation (Ch 7).
**How**: involve learners with disabilities in design and testing; generate alternative formats (text-to-speech, speech-to-text); make font, contrast, and navigation customisable; provide multimodal feedback and in-task support; verify interoperability with screen readers, braille displays, and alternative input before purchase.
**Trade-offs**: interoperability is where procurement usually fails — test it before signing, not at rollout.
