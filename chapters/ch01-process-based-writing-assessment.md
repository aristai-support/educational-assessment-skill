# Chapter 1: Using Process-Based Pedagogical Assessment Practices to Challenge Generative AI in the Writing Classroom

## Core Idea
Assess how students got to the text, not the text itself — because GenAI can only deliver a polished product, and a product-focused rubric is exactly what it defeats. Process-over-product assessment simultaneously blocks the cheap AI shortcut and opens space for students' own languages and dialects, which product-focused Standard American English grading suppresses.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Process over product pedagogy** (Murray, 1972): grade the stages of composing, not the finished artifact.
  - When to use: any writing-bearing course where GenAI can plausibly produce a passing final draft.
  - How: build every unit as a sequence — prewriting/invention, drafting, peer response, revision, reflection — and assign points to each stage. Murray's three stages are prewriting (he argues ~85% of the effort: finding subject, spotting audience, choosing form, research, note-making, outlining), writing (the fast, frightening first draft), and rewriting (editing plus reconsideration of subject, form, and audience).

- **Cognitive Process Theory of Writing** (Flower & Hayes): writing is hierarchical and recursive, not linear.
  - When to use: when designing the *shape* of a process sequence — it justifies loops back to invention rather than a one-way pipeline.
  - How: treat three elements as always in play — the task environment, the writer's long-term memory, and the writing processes themselves (planning, translating, reviewing), with constant movement between them.

- **Critiquing Generative AI Output assignment**: students prompt a GenAI tool, record prompt and output verbatim, then critique it along three axes.
  - When to use: as the *first* assignment of a term, to establish both the process system and a shared AI vocabulary.
  - How: critique on (1) **accessibility** — the experience of using the tool, (2) **accuracy/output** — what was sought, what was useful, what was wrong or confusing, (3) **style/tone** — does it sound like you, do you like it. Each axis is compared against assigned readings so the critique is argued, not merely felt. Deliverable is a short essay with the prompt and output embedded, plus a Works Cited including the AI tool.

- **Extended Resources page** (adapted from Blair Van Dam): a disclosure appendix attached to *every* essay, replacing the bare Works Cited.
  - When to use: on every graded writing submission, all term — not once.
  - How: students list every resource used, not just cited sources — GenAI tools, the Writing Center, friends, family, tutors — and for each answer four questions: what the resource was and how they found/accessed it; why they used it; **how** they used it (specifically); anything else the instructor should know. Links to actual prompts and outputs are encouraged.

## Key Concepts
- **Product-driven assessment**: grading that credits only the polished final text, which GenAI can supply directly.
- **Standard American English (SAE)**: the privileged dialect that GenAI reproduces by default, reinforcing a linguistic hierarchy.
- **Myth of linguistic homogeneity** (Matsuda): the tacit assumption that the class audience is white, native-speaking, and monolingual.
- **SLMN** (Horner & Selfe): "single, uniform ('standard') language and modality" — the narrow convention set most FYW assignments quietly enforce.
- **Hidden curriculum** (McLaren): the rules of conduct, classroom organization, and informal procedures that shape students alongside the stated outcomes.
- **Writing apprehension**: the fear of being red-penned that drives students toward outsourcing — to essay markets before, to GenAI now.
- **AI as cognitive equalizer**: a multilingual student drafting in a home language, translating, then using AI to edit — ideas are the student's, and the cognitive load is higher, not lower.

## Mental Models
- **Think of a thesis-free AI essay as a symptom, not a crime.** Students who outsource are usually apprehensive, not lazy; AI-written essays typically fail on assignment fit anyway. Design for the apprehension.
- **Use disclosure instead of detection.** You cannot reliably detect AI; you can make honest reporting the lower-friction path by grading the disclosure itself.
- **Treat GenAI's fluency as a teaching artifact.** Its prose is the status-quo academic register made visible. When a student replaces their own voice with AI phrasing "because it's better," that is the lesson — run it as a discussion about who gets to sound authoritative.
- **Use a portable process system, not per-assignment scaffolding.** The value is that students meet the same steps (unpack the prompt → outline → draft → peer review → reflect → disclose) on every assignment, so the system, not the topic, is what they internalize.

## Anti-patterns
- **Asking AI to summarize an assigned reading, then critiquing the summary**: current tools produce perfectly serviceable summaries, so the critique has nothing to bite on. Broaden the prompt to student-chosen topics where output weakness is visible.
- **Bolting the disclosure page on as a low-stakes afterthought**: students read it as busywork and write nothing usable. Integrate it into the assignment sheet and the process, and weight it enough to matter (without making it alarming).
- **Accepting "I used ChatGPT for my summary paragraph" as sufficient disclosure**: it names the use but not the integrity work — verification, revision, ownership. Model critical reflective disclosure explicitly.
- **Presenting GenAI without explaining what it is**: a cursory instructor gloss leaves students believing the tool "thinks." Bring in someone who can explain the mechanism.

## Worked Example
A first-unit sequence in a four-credit developmental writing course (ENG 100), spanning roughly the first weeks of the term:

1. **Weeks 1–2 — establish process before AI.** Diagnostic, surveys, and readings that frame writing as recursive: Murray's process-over-product essay and Lamott's "Shitty First Drafts." Goal: dislodge the belief that writing is a skill you either have or don't.
2. **Week 2 — open the AI question.** Whole-class discussion: what is it, what experience do you have with it. Students then read the assignment sheet, the rubric, and an instructor-written model of the essay they will write.
3. **Collaborative unpacking.** Jigsaw groups explore AI on a shared anonymous whiteboard (originally Jamboard, later Google Slides) — anonymity matters because students afraid of "using it wrong" will not speak up otherwise.
4. **Prompt interrogation.** Every student completes an "Understanding Your Writing Assignment" activity — combing the assignment sheet for audience, voice, purpose, source requirements, and documentation style. Done in class with each answer discussed for the first assignment; repeated independently for every later assignment.
5. **Positioning readings.** Two short pieces representing opposing takes on student AI use, which become the comparison points for the critique.
6. **Drafting in and out of class**, with light homework increments so the instructor is present for questions, then a **peer response workshop** and final submission — 1–3 pages, MLA, minimum two sources (one article plus the AI tool), submitted as an editable doc so revision history is visible.

The rubric is criterion-weighted rather than holistic: MLA formatting; presence and correct in-text citation of the AI prompt and output; Works Cited completeness; an introduction that situates the source article (author, title, year, publication); a debatable thesis focused on the AI critique; and at least three organized body paragraphs with topic sentences and worked examples. Note that the criteria reward *doing the process visibly* — showing the prompt, citing the machine, staging the argument — more than they reward prose polish.

## Key Takeaways
1. Make each stage of the process a graded artifact; a product-only rubric hands GenAI the win by default.
2. Put a disclosure appendix on every assignment and give it real point weight — the disclosure is the assessment.
3. Use a single portable process system across all assignments so students transfer it rather than relearn it.
4. Start the term with a hands-on AI critique so the tool's limits are established by the students, not asserted by you.
5. Ask students *how* they used a resource, specifically — the "how" is where integrity and learning are visible.
6. Frame AI fluency as an entry point to talk about which Englishes get privileged; this is a linguistic-justice move, not just an integrity move.
7. Watch for students overwriting their own voice with AI phrasing because it "sounds better" — intercept that moment, it is the teachable one.
