# Chapter 9: Exploring Generative AI Impact on Assessment and Evaluation in Management Education

## Core Idea
Generative AI leaves no forensic trace, so detection-based integrity policy is
already lost; the workable response is to redesign assessment around what AI
makes newly affordable — adaptive, continuous, multimodal, real-time-feedback
evaluation — and to write institutional policy fast rather than wait for tooling
to catch up. This chapter is a broad capability survey rather than a tight
methodology; its value is the taxonomy of AI assessment affordances plus
strategy set, not deep empirical evidence.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Five AI Assessment Affordances in Management Education**: realistic
  simulations and scenarios; enhanced collaboration (AI-composed teams);
  bias reduction; continuous and multimodal assessment; automated grading.
  - When to use: auditing an existing assessment portfolio to see which
    affordances you have adopted and which you have left unused.
  - How: for each existing assessment, ask which of the five it exploits. A
    portfolio using only automated grading has captured the cheapest and least
    pedagogically interesting affordance.

- **Six Technology-Enabler Properties of Conversational AI**: personalized
  learning and feedback; instant feedback; scalability; 24/7 availability;
  customized assessments; learning analytics and data-driven insights.
  - When to use: justifying (or challenging) a specific AI deployment — each
    property is a distinct value claim that should be separately evidenced.
  - How: name which property the deployment is buying. "Scalability" (MOOC-size
    cohorts, thousands of submissions) is a different business case from
    "instant feedback" (shortening the correction loop) and needs different
    evidence.

- **Five Strategies of AI Usage for Assessment and Evaluation**: adaptive
  testing; automated assessment scoring; language proficiency evaluation;
  emotion and engagement monitoring; plagiarism detection.
  - When to use: choosing a concrete implementation, not a philosophy.
  - How: adaptive testing calibrates item difficulty from prior responses to
    hold the learner near their edge of competence. Automated scoring evaluates
    written work on more than grammar — lexical richness, argumentative
    cohesion, topical relevance. Engagement monitoring reads non-verbal signals
    in virtual classes; treat it as the most ethically loaded of the five.

## Key Concepts
- **Adaptive testing**: assessment that tunes item difficulty in real time to
  the learner's demonstrated skill level, using prior responses as the signal.
- **Continuous assessment**: monitoring the learning trajectory across time so
  intervention is preemptive rather than post-mortem.
- **Multimodal assessment**: evaluating across several media forms rather than
  a single written artifact, to capture more competency dimensions.
- **Automated assessment scoring**: NLP-driven evaluation of written work,
  valued for consistency and speed rather than depth of judgment.
- **Standardized evaluation protocol**: algorithmically uniform grading intended
  to remove rater subjectivity tied to race, gender, or socioeconomic background.
- **AI-driven simulation**: a virtual managerial environment that assesses
  navigation of complexity rather than recall of concepts.
- **Learning analytics**: systematic reading of interaction data to surface
  recurring misconceptions and difficulty hotspots across a cohort.
- **Detection evasion**: the property that separates generative AI from prior
  ed-tech — usage leaves no discernible trace, which is why the chapter treats
  policy, not detection, as the lever.

## Mental Models
- Think of AI-driven simulation as **assessing navigation, not recall**: the
  score comes from how the learner handles unforeseen contingencies inside the
  scenario, not from whether the final decision matched a key.
- Use **scalability as the trigger for automation**, not quality: automate
  grading when cohort size makes the manual feedback loop too slow to be
  formative, and reinvest the reclaimed time in the higher-order teaching that
  only a human can do.
- Treat **plagiarism detection as a losing arms race**. The chapter frames
  detector-versus-generator as an ongoing contest with no stable winner; policy
  and task design are the durable moves.
- Think of **bias as bidirectional**: AI removes rater subjectivity but imports
  training-data bias. Neither the human nor the machine path is neutral by
  default.

## Anti-patterns
- **Betting integrity policy on detection tools**: generative output evades
  detection by design, so an integrity regime resting on catching users is
  building on ground that keeps moving.
- **Adopting AI grading for speed alone**: the reclaimed instructor time is the
  actual return; if it is not redirected into discussion, guidance, and richer
  task design, the change is cost-shifting rather than improvement.
- **Treating algorithmic scoring as automatically fair**: standardization
  removes one bias source and installs another via training data. Fairness is a
  claim to be tested, not a property conferred by automation.
- **Deploying engagement/emotion monitoring without a privacy position**:
  reading facial expression and body language in virtual classes generates the
  most sensitive data in the whole stack, with the weakest consent story.
- **Over-reliance and the widening digital divide**: AI capability accrues to
  the already-resourced; unfunded adoption widens rather than closes gaps.

## Worked Example
The chapter's clearest mechanic is **the continuous-assessment loop replacing
the terminal exam**, exemplified by an institutional LMS that assesses
throughout rather than at the end.

The redesign runs like this: instead of a single high-stakes summative event,
the course instruments every graded and ungraded interaction. Analytics run
continuously over that interaction stream, surfacing per-student progress
signals and cohort-level difficulty hotspots. Feedback returns to the student
immediately, so a misconception is corrected while the material is still live
rather than after the grade is fixed. The instructor reads the same analytics
in aggregate and recalibrates instruction mid-course — the assessment doubles
as an instructional-refinement instrument.

Two structural consequences follow. First, the unit of assessment shifts from
the artifact to the trajectory: what is being evaluated is the shape of the
learning curve, not one sample from it. Second, the instructor's marginal hour
moves from grading to intervention, which is the only way the scale economics
work at MOOC or large-lecture size.

## Key Takeaways
1. Write AI policy before you have perfect tooling — accessibility and low cost
   mean adoption is already ahead of governance.
2. Automate the assessment work that is high-volume and low-judgment, and spend
   the recovered time on the judgment-heavy work.
3. Assess trajectories, not artifacts: continuous instrumentation gives earlier
   intervention points than any end-of-term instrument.
4. Prefer simulation tasks that force navigation of contingency; they resist AI
   substitution better than knowledge-recall items and assess more of what the
   discipline actually needs.
5. Name the specific affordance you are buying (scale, speed, personalization,
   bias reduction) — each carries a different evidentiary burden and different
   failure mode.
6. Pair every automation decision with an equity check: privacy exposure,
   access to the tool, and training-data bias all fall unevenly.
