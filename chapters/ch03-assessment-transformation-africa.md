# Chapter 3: Educational Assessments in the Age of Generative AI: Transforming Educational Assessment in Africa

## Core Idea
Generative AI can genuinely improve formative assessment — personalised feedback, adaptive pathways, early-warning analytics — but it should not be trusted with high-stakes summative judgment, and in African contexts the digital divide means poorly-planned adoption widens the inequity it promises to close.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Formative/summative split as the deployment boundary**: use GAI where it is strong, keep humans where it is not.
  - When to use: any decision about whether to let an AI tool touch a given assessment.
  - How: formative — real-time feedback, adaptive practice, progress monitoring, early intervention — is where GAI adds the most value and errors are recoverable. Summative — final grades, national examinations — stays under faculty oversight; the argument against outsourcing here is fairness, bias, and accuracy, not efficiency.

- **Twelve-challenge audit for AI-enhanced assessment**: run a candidate tool against all twelve before adoption — (1) data privacy and security, (2) algorithmic bias and fairness, (3) impact on traditional pedagogy including teacher de-skilling, (4) technology-enabled cheating and plagiarism, (5) loss of the human/social dimension of assessment, (6) transparency and accountability in AI decision-making, (7) digital divide and inequitable access, (8) cost and integration with existing systems, (9) ethics and intellectual property, (10) staff training and technical expertise, (11) AI's limits on complex skills (creativity, problem-solving, collaboration, affective and psychomotor domains), (12) student motivation and engagement.

- **Institutional governance recommendations** (after UNESCO): institutional auditing of AI algorithms and outputs on a regular cycle — covering ethical soundness of algorithm and data, accuracy of output, protection of user data, and automatic filtering of inappropriate content; validating proportionality and safeguarding well-being, with policies that classify and validate AI systems against ethical frameworks, especially for vulnerable groups; assessing long-term impacts on creativity and critical thinking; and age appropriateness limits on independent use.

- **Mitigation set for equitable AI assessment** (after Owan et al.): transparent algorithms built on diverse datasets; personalised and adaptive assessments including open-ended and performance-based tasks; ongoing educator professional development; collaborating with students by gathering their feedback on the tools; accessibility by design for assistive technologies; human oversight verifying AI-driven judgments; regular re-evaluation of tools for effectiveness and privacy compliance; robust data security under regimes such as GDPR and FERPA; and educating students and parents on what data is collected and how it is used.

- **The lecturer's four persisting roles under AI**: assessment design (aligning tools to curriculum and learning objectives), result interpretation (contextual feedback AI cannot give), ethical oversight (fairness, validity, reliability), and continuous improvement (using AI-generated data to refine teaching).

## Key Concepts
- **Generative AI (GAI)**: models that produce text, image, audio, or video content from natural-language prompts, defined by adaptability, autonomy, and learning from data.
- **Adaptive testing / adaptive learning**: systems that change item difficulty and content in real time based on learner responses.
- **Intelligent Tutoring System (ITS)**: an AI tutor delivering personalised feedback and support, valuable for closing learning gaps.
- **Black-box problem**: AI grading decisions that cannot be explained, so a student cannot learn from the result and an educator cannot audit it.
- **Algorithmic bias**: societal inequities present in training data being reproduced or amplified in grades and feedback — including narrowing the range of answers judged acceptable.
- **Digital divide**: unequal access to devices, connectivity, and tools that turns any AI benefit into a stratifying force.
- **Guardrails**: developer-side content filters that are real but not foolproof, so educators must teach validation of AI output rather than trust it.
- **Predictive analytics for at-risk students**: using attendance, engagement, and performance data to trigger early intervention.
- **Human-centric learning**: the emotional and social support, and the cue-reading, that a human assessor supplies and an AI cannot.

## Mental Models
- **Use AI where a wrong answer is cheap and reversible.** Formative feedback tolerates error because the student iterates; a final grade does not.
- **Think of an AI grade as an unexplainable verdict.** A human examiner can justify a decision; if a tool cannot, treat its output as a signal to a human, never as the decision itself.
- **Deploy on the assumption that access is unequal.** Assume the tool will reach well-resourced students first. If that assumption makes the design unfair, the design is unfair.
- **Treat teacher de-skilling as a real cost.** Over-delegating lesson planning and grading produces more generic teaching, so the efficiency gain has a hidden charge against it.

## Worked Example
Reading the case studies as a comparative decision aid — each region shows what a deployment buys and what it costs:

| Deployment | What it delivers | What it costs / limits |
|---|---|---|
| **China** — platforms processing behavioural and question data to produce personalised performance reports and recommendation-driven study plans | Tailored assessment, data-driven insight into strengths and weaknesses | Heavy dependence on very large datasets raises privacy exposure; the model does not port to regions without that infrastructure |
| **USA** — adaptive learning platforms that adjust difficulty dynamically per learner | Individualised paths; frees class time for students needing support | Focused on cognitive/procedural skills; weak on higher-order thinking, creativity, problem-solving |
| **UK** — AI-enhanced similarity and feedback tooling, plus national investment in teacher-facing AI resources | Faster marking turnaround; scalable real-time feedback | Feedback lacks nuance and empathy; bias persists on subjective tasks like essays |
| **Singapore** — AI progress monitoring flagging underperformance and disengagement to teachers | Proactive early intervention; personalisation at system scale | Over-reliance reduces human contact; less attention to emotional and social development |
| **Africa** — SMS-and-AI personalised learning reaching primary learners without smartphones; adaptive modules where schooling is not accessible; AI-assisted teacher training and performance feedback; pandemic-era remote learning engagement analytics | Reaches learners traditional schooling misses; bridges gaps in underserved regions; builds teacher capacity | Connectivity and device access remain the binding constraint; without infrastructure investment the tools amplify existing disparity |

The generalisable read: every deployment trades human contact and contextual nuance for scale and speed. The African case is distinctive because the constraint is upstream of pedagogy — SMS-based delivery is the design insight, because it makes the intervention work on the infrastructure that actually exists rather than the infrastructure the tool assumes.

## Key Takeaways
1. Deploy GAI in formative assessment; keep summative and high-stakes grading under faculty oversight.
2. Audit any tool against bias, privacy, transparency, cost, accessibility, and staff capability before adoption — not after.
3. Demand explainability: if neither student nor educator can see why a grade was given, the tool cannot serve the educational purpose.
4. Design for the infrastructure that exists (the SMS lesson), not the one the vendor assumes.
5. Avoid over-relying on AI-detection tools — accuracy is poor and they create an adversarial classroom.
6. Prefer redesigning assignments toward iterative, scaffolded deliverables over policing AI use.
7. Teach students to use AI for revising, critiquing, and brainstorming — AI literacy is now part of the curriculum, not a distraction from it.
8. Budget for educator training; a tool nobody can interpret produces flawed assessments regardless of its quality.
9. Build ethical frameworks that reflect local cultural and social context rather than importing them wholesale.
