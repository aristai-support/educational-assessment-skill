# Chapter 4: Developing AI-Powered Prompts for First-Year Writing Courses

## Core Idea
Stop asking whether students may use AI and start building the AI interaction into the assessment itself — a dual framework (balanced integration × AI literacy) turns a thesis assignment into a tutored, staged dialogue where the graded evidence is the interaction log and the reflections, not the thesis sentence.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Framework for Balanced AI Integration** (built on Chowdhury Niloy et al., 2024) — four tenets governing how AI enters the curriculum:
  - **Setting Clear Guidelines** — state in the assignment prompt itself which tasks permit AI and which require independent work.
  - **Learning Process Over Product** — grade a log of research, thinking, and reflection rather than a final draft.
  - **Encouraging Critical Thinking** — require students to interrogate AI output, analysing its strengths and limitations against course material rather than accepting it.
  - **Feedback and Reflection** — require students to reflect on their own AI use and rate the content they receive, building metacognition and self-regulated learning.
  - When to use: as the structural layer whenever designing an assessment that will involve AI at all.

- **Framework for Cultivating AI Literacy** (built on Cardon et al., 2023) — four competencies students must develop, the "4 A's":
  - **Application** — understanding the range of generative AI tools including their strengths and weaknesses, so the right capability is chosen for the task.
  - **Authenticity** — prioritising genuine human communication over AI output; infusing AI-assisted content with personal voice, cultural nuance, and context.
  - **Accountability** — owning the accuracy and appropriateness of AI output (fact-checking is only part of it) and using AI equitably.
  - **Agency** — people retaining control to make their own choices; viewing AI as augmenting rather than replacing independent reasoning.
  - When to use: as the skills layer. Cross it with the four integration tenets to generate concrete task requirements — the 4×4 grid *is* the assessment design method.

- **AI-enabled vs AI-powered assessment** — the load-bearing distinction.
  - **AI-enabled**: students are permitted to use AI while completing an otherwise traditional assignment.
  - **AI-powered**: AI is integrated into the learning process itself — adaptive, dialogic, giving real-time guidance through the assessment journey, and the interaction is what gets assessed.
  - When to use: whenever you catch yourself writing an AI *policy* instead of an AI *design*.

- **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) as a pedagogical device**: break complex problems into a series of visible logical steps.
  - When to use: to demystify a process that expert writers perform tacitly.
  - How: make the reasoning steps of proficient writers explicit so novices can internalise the logical flow; use CoT-structured prompting to generate stage-appropriate guidance and scaffold students through complex writing tasks.

- **Process approach to writing** (as the pedagogical substrate): break writing into brainstorming, drafting, revising, editing; give clear guidelines per stage, tailored actionable feedback, collaborative peer activity, and achievable milestones. Each of the four Balanced Integration tenets maps onto a feature of this approach — clear guidelines onto structured stages, process-over-product onto formative feedback at each stage, critical thinking onto revision and peer review, feedback-and-reflection onto metacognition.

## Key Concepts
- **AI literacy**: the capacity to evaluate AI technologies and their outputs, communicate and collaborate with AI, and use AI tools across settings — treated here as parallel to traditional literacy, not subordinate to it.
- **Novice advantage**: productivity/quality gains from generative AI are largest for the least-skilled workers (roughly 14% average, ~34% for novices) — and first-year writers are, by definition, novices.
- **Academic disengagement**: missed deadlines and under-preparation that push students toward shortcuts; a predictor of AI dependency.
- **Confidence as the lever**: low academic self-confidence produces disengagement behaviours (not asking questions, not seeking help); inexperienced writers are also the most enthusiastic about writing as their route into college.
- **Human–AI trust relationship**: users trust systems whose outputs they can interpret; explainability drives a more positive interaction.
- **Thesis as action, not artifact**: treating the thesis as a process that defines what the student is thinking and the scope of the writing to come.
- **Interaction log**: the full transcript of the student–AI dialogue, which becomes the primary assessable evidence.

## Mental Models
- **Use the 4×4 grid as an assignment generator.** For each integration tenet, ask what Application, Authenticity, Accountability, and Agency each require here. Sixteen cells, each producing a concrete task requirement — that is the design work.
- **Think of confidence as the actual outcome variable.** First-year students reach for AI because they are overwhelmed and unsure, not because they are cynical. An assessment that raises confidence removes the motive.
- **Treat hallucination risk as a designed-in teaching moment.** Accountability and Application requirements (fact-check, compare tools, identify gaps) convert the model's unreliability from a hazard into the exercise.

## Anti-patterns
- **Off-the-shelf thesis generators**: automated, product-only, clunky, no nuance — the student learns nothing and leaves with no grasp of the process.
- **The detect-and-report posture**: treats a tool students already use as contraband, forfeits the chance to model effective use, and produces an adversarial classroom.
- **Merely permitting AI ("AI-enabled")**: a traditional assignment with an AI clause changes nothing about what is measured.
- **Outsourcing content generation**: the shortcut that bypasses the iterative critical thinking and knowledge construction that makes the education transformative — grammar and paraphrase help is a different case from content generation.
- **Assuming equal starting literacy**: students arrive with unequal AI access and fluency; AI-powered assessment without literacy workshops or access programmes disadvantages some populations.

## Worked Example
**The thesis accelerator** — a four-step AI tutor interaction replacing a one-shot thesis submission. The AI is instructed to act as a tutor: guide thesis development, challenge students to build their own knowledge, use direct instruction plus open-ended questions, hints, and examples, surface misconceptions, stay encouraging and practical, and hold high expectations.

- **Step 1 — Begin tutoring (independent work).** The tutor helps the student pick a topic and refines it through questioning until the student can state it clearly. Brief answers get pushed for elaboration. *Assesses:* baseline ability to articulate ideas without AI content generation.
- **Step 2 — Explore knowledge (independent, AI-assisted).** The student lists what they already know and what they want to learn, and explains how their own experience shapes their approach to the topic. *Assesses:* prior knowledge, and — via the experience question — Authenticity.
- **Step 3 — Develop the thesis (collaborative with AI).** The tutor guides but explicitly does not write the thesis. It offers two or three candidate thesis statements; the student compares them against their own idea and analyses strengths and weaknesses; the student then refines their thesis using those insights; the student fact-checks key claims against credible sources and reports what changed. *Assesses:* Application (tool selection and comparison), Accountability (verification), Agency (choosing against AI suggestions).
- **Step 4 — Reflect (independent work).** What was challenging, what was new versus familiar; how AI helped or hindered their thinking; how they maintained authenticity while using AI input. *Assesses:* metacognition and AI literacy directly.

**How it is graded.** Not on the thesis sentence. The instructor reviews the whole interaction log plus a portfolio containing AI outputs, the student's modifications, research notes, and stage reflections — including an evolution document showing initial AI suggestions, the student's reasoning, and a justification of why the final thesis was chosen over the AI-suggested alternatives. A closing meta-cognitive reflection asks how the student's approach to AI in academic work changed across the process, and the reflections feed class discussion and peer learning.

**Why each framework tenet is visible.** Clear Guidelines: the prompt states which steps are independent and which are collaborative. Process Over Product: staged submissions with reflections at each point rather than one final thesis. Critical Thinking: comparing AI-generated arguments, outlining their logical structure, identifying bias or gaps in AI research summaries. Feedback and Reflection: journals rating tool effectiveness, peer discussion of maintaining authentic voice, and logs recording and justifying decisions — especially divergences from AI advice.

## Key Takeaways
1. Design AI-powered assessments, not AI-enabled ones — if AI is only permitted, nothing about what you measure has changed.
2. Cross the four integration tenets with the four literacy competencies; the resulting grid produces concrete task requirements.
3. Grade the interaction log, the modifications, and the reflections — the artifact is the least informative part.
4. Alternate independent and collaborative phases so you can see baseline capability and AI-mediated capability separately.
5. Require students to justify choosing their own thesis over the AI's; that justification is where Agency becomes assessable.
6. Build fact-checking into the task; it converts hallucination risk into the Accountability exercise.
7. Target confidence explicitly for first-year writers — visible scaffolding and staged wins do more than grammar rules at the start.
8. Rubrics must be rewritten for process, critical thinking, and appropriate tool use — and instructors need ongoing training to keep pace.
