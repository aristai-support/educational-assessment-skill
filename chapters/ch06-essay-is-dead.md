# Chapter 6: The Essay Is Dead (And Other Thoughts About Assessing in the Era of AI)

## Core Idea
You cannot prevent, detect, or talk students out of GenAI use, so the only
sustainable move is to redesign the assessment: deconstruct the essay into its
constituent skills, then rebuild assessments that target the skills LLMs are bad at.
Despite the provocative title, this is the most *tool-dense* chapter in the volume —
five named taxonomies plus a full redesign procedure.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Five "Can'ts"** (the argument that forces redesign): we can't ignore GenAI, can't prevent it, can't detect it, can't convince students to avoid it, and can't easily engineer workarounds. Therefore: lean in.
  - When to use: as the opening move in any faculty conversation still arguing about detection.
  - How: address each in order; each has its own evidence (detector false positives, ~50–75% of students will use AI even when prohibited, prompt-obfuscation tools).
- **Centaur / Cyborg collaboration (Mollick)**: two distinct human–AI working relationships.
  - Centaur: a visible seam — human does task A, AI does task B, division of labor is explicit. Use when you want to assess the human half in isolation.
  - Cyborg: no seam — continuous back-and-forth, human seeds a sentence and AI extends it, human redirects. Use when the learning objective *is* the iterative collaboration.
  - How: state which model the assignment requires, and grade the corresponding artifact (the handoff decisions for Centaur; the transcript for Cyborg).
- **LLM vs. Human relative strengths table**: LLMs — summarizing, brainstorming, answering questions, classifying text, outlining, first drafts, shallow analysis. Humans — critiquing, judging, ethics, evaluating, social interaction, deep analysis.
  - When to use: deciding what an assignment may delegate and what it must reserve.
  - How: don't abandon the LLM column — *reframe* it. Instead of teaching summarization, teach critical evaluation of AI-generated summaries.
- **Skill deconstruction via backward design (Wiggins & McTighe)**: before replacing an essay, enumerate why you assigned it.
  - When to use: any time you are tempted to swap "essay" for "video presentation."
  - How: the humanities example decomposes into six skills — (1) ideate/brainstorm, (2) formulate a central thesis, (3) create sub-claims, (4) arrange sub-claims into a cohesive (not merely stacked) argument, (5) draft strong paragraphs with clear topic sentences, (6) connect evidence to sub-claims. Extend the list using Bloom's action verbs, then design an alternative that hits the same list.
- **Bloom's Digital Taxonomy applied to assessment design** (5 steps): (1) analyze which taxonomy levels your current assessments actually address; (2) elevate lower-level tasks into higher-order ones; (3) emphasize creation and evaluation; (4) incorporate digital skills; (5) assess process, not just product.
  - When to use: auditing an existing course whose assessments are recall-heavy.
- **Aiken's Taxonomy of higher-order MCQs** (5 item types): Premise-to-Consequence; Multiple Premises (several inputs combine to one conclusion); Classification (which category does this belong to); Dual Propositions (two T/F statements — answer is one, both, or neither); Relationship Between Concepts (analogy form).
  - When to use: you need proctorable, software-safeguarded assessment but still want critical thinking.
- **Haladyna's expanded MCQ taxonomy** (5 formats): embedded-blank items; matching; extended matching (a scenario stem with more options than questions); complex multiple choice (select a combination, e.g. "1 and 3"); **context-dependent item sets** (a stimulus — passage, chart, diagram — followed by a series of items).
  - When to use: context-dependent item sets are the format the authors single out as easiest to use for measuring application, analysis, and evaluation.

## Key Concepts
- **Recognizing vs. detecting AI text**: experienced faculty can often *recognize* LLM prose, but cannot produce the verifiable source evidence that plagiarism detection supplied — so recognition is not adjudicable.
- **False-positive trap of AI detectors**: detectors systematically flag "average" writing, penalizing beginning writers, non-native speakers, and anyone using grammar-improvement software.
- **Obfuscation tooling**: paraphrasers and style-matching draft generators that defeat both plagiarism and AI detection; their existence makes detection a losing race.
- **Burstiness and perplexity**: rarity of word choice and unpredictability of text — teachable concepts that double as a critical-reading exercise for students learning to distinguish human from AI writing.
- **Competency-based assessment**: graduating students on demonstrated mastery; the authors flag the atrophy risk — fundamentals delegated to GenAI may never be acquired.
- **Modality equity problem**: online students have AI access and face-to-face students in a proctored room do not, so the same course produces incommensurable grades.

## Mental Models
- Think of GenAI as **the calculator moment**: resistance was rational and lost; the resolution was not banning arithmetic but relocating assessment to higher-order problem solving.
- Use **"what skill was the essay a proxy for?"** whenever you feel loss about a dying assignment — the essay was never the objective, it was the instrument.
- Think of AI-detection software as **an opinion, not evidence** — it produces a confidence score with no verifiable source, which is why institutions are disabling it and declining to prosecute on it alone.
- Use **process artifacts as the assessment surface** when the product is forgeable: chat transcripts, tracked changes, research logs, and revision history are all things AI does not hand you for free.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reverting to in-class timed handwritten essays**: timed assessments are less valid, reliable, inclusive, and equitable than untimed ones; they assess only limited forms of competence, disadvantage non-native speakers and neurodivergent students, and are impossible for the majority of students now taking online courses.
- **Reusing a five-year-old prompt**: familiar prompts are exactly what LLMs answer best; recycling one is an invitation.
- **Word-swap redesign** (replacing "essay" with "video presentation" and changing nothing else): produces a different medium with the same unexamined objectives.
- **Prosecuting integrity cases on a detector score alone**: demonstrated false positives, and institutions are already refusing these cases.
- **Requiring specific tools for digital assignments**: faculty do not need to be technologists — specifying the tool imports a support burden and removes the tool-selection skill students should be practicing.

## Worked Example
**The biology essay, redesigned.**

*Before*: "Write a 2,000-word essay on genetic modification." Now fully answerable by
an LLM from a single paste, with no engagement with the course at all.

*After*: "Use your preferred AI platform to generate a 2,000-word essay on genetic
modification. Copy the output into a document. Then, using track changes, insert
quotations and information from this week's assigned readings and your lecture notes
that either support or challenge the ideas in the AI-generated essay."

Why it works: the AI output becomes the *object* of assessment rather than the
submission. The graded artifact is the annotation layer — evidence selection,
source-to-claim mapping, and detection of the AI's characteristic failures (outdated
information, broad-and-shallow treatment, absent course-specific content). The
instructor's subject expertise is exactly what is needed to grade it, and no detector
is involved because AI use is stipulated.

*Then extend it into a workshop*: students bring their annotated documents to class,
pair or group up, and synthesize their individual annotations into a new shared
outline. Freeing students from drafting buys class time for deeper engagement with
the material; the instructor circulates, corrects misconceptions, and records the
questions students ask — which becomes evidence of how understanding moved between
the workshop and the final submission. The comparison the authors invite: is this not
a better instrument for "teaching students to think critically" than the original
essay ever was?

## Key Takeaways
1. Stop investing in detection; redirect that effort into redesign. Detectors produce opinions, punish average and non-native writers, and are losing to obfuscation tools.
2. Before replacing any assignment, list the constituent skills it was measuring; design the replacement against the list, not against the format.
3. Make AI output the object of analysis rather than the submission — annotate it, challenge it with course sources, critique its summaries.
4. Name the collaboration model (Centaur or Cyborg) in the prompt, and grade the artifact that model produces.
5. If you need proctorable assessment, write higher-order MCQs using Aiken's five types and Haladyna's context-dependent item sets rather than reverting to timed handwriting.
6. Assess process alongside product: research logs, tracked changes, chat transcripts, reflections on choices.
8. Keep a competency check on fundamentals; leaning in creates a real skill-atrophy risk that only mastery measurement will catch.
