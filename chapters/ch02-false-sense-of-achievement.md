# Chapter 2: The False Sense of Achievement: Navigating Academic Integrity and Assessment Challenges of GenAI

## Core Idea
GenAI lets students who have not learned produce work that passes, which inflates grades, decouples credentials from competence, and gives students a false sense of achievement they only discover is hollow at exams or on the job. The fix is not better detection — it is assessment redesign toward authentic, interactive, and incremental formats, backed by institutional policy.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Authentic Assessment as the governing design principle** (drawing on Wiewiora & Kowalkiewicz): assessments grounded in module-specific context and content, meaningful to the student, tied to real-world problems, and carrying a reflective element from experiential learning.
  - When to use: as the default design principle for any assessment you would otherwise set as an essay or problem set — and ideally applied across a whole programme, not one module.
  - How: anchor the task in this cohort's context; require the student's own experience or data; add a reflective component; pair it with an interactive or multimedia delivery format.

- **Assessment-type × Bloom's mapping**: a decision matrix for choosing formats in the GenAI era. Reconstructed:

| Format | Main benefit | Main risk | GenAI adaptation | Bloom's level targeted |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Creative/multimedia projects (video, posters) | Engages multiple modalities; shows practical skill | Needs tech access and skill; slow to build and mark | Rubrics that score creativity, technical execution, and content comprehension separately | Create, Apply |
| Written essays / research papers | Deep topic exploration; synthesis and evaluation | Most exposed to AI substitution; hard to verify originality | Require personal reflection and lived experience AI cannot supply; use detection as a supplement only | Understand, Evaluate |
| Case studies and simulations | Applies theory; builds analytical skill | AI can generate plausible solutions | Require multiple candidate solutions plus justification of the choice; add oral defence | Analyse, Apply |
| Group projects | Collaboration, project management | Individual contribution is hard to isolate | Peer evaluation plus presentation and reflective components | Apply, Evaluate |
| Viva / oral defence | Assesses real-time understanding; strongest AI resistance | Stressful; time-expensive; examiner bias | Record sessions for moderation; use structured rubrics for consistency | Understand, Evaluate |
| Presentations | Communication skill; visible synthesis | AI can draft the content | Rubric weighting delivery and engagement; mandatory Q&A | Create, Understand |
| Peer review / self-assessment / reflection | Personal judgment AI cannot replicate | Students may use AI to structure the reflection | Require personal examples and reasoning behind each evaluative choice | Evaluate, Analyse |
| Quizzes | Fast recall check | AI answers them instantly and invisibly | Adaptive quizzes; follow-up items demanding explanation or application | Remember, Apply |

- **Incremental / iterative assessment**: assess progress in real time rather than the endpoint.
  - When to use: for any semester-long or high-weight deliverable.
  - How: require staged submissions — outlines, drafts, progress reports — reviewed as they arrive; add in-class assignments to test immediate understanding; add oral defence of written work so the student must justify their own text live.

- **Policy stack for institutions**: ten coordinated measures — explicit AI-use rules with worked examples of acceptable and unacceptable use; cross-disciplinary digital literacy programmes; detection tooling (as one input, continuously updated); assessment redesign toward higher-order thinking; incentives for originality; honour codes updated to name AI misuse plus faculty training and clear violation procedures; collaboration with AI developers; advocacy for national/international regulation; ethics teaching embedded in the curriculum; and a standing committee that monitors and revises the policies with student and faculty feedback.

## Key Concepts
- **False sense of achievement**: a student's belief that they have mastered material when they have only mastered operating the tool.
- **AI-driven grade inflation**: awarding higher marks for AI-polished work than the same student's unaided work would earn, until grades stop discriminating.
- **Barely-passable submission problem**: students who would previously have failed (and been given a resit or remediation) now pass, so the failure signal — and the learning intervention it triggers — never fires.
- **Erosion of academic standards**: grades ceasing to represent mastery, devaluing the institution's credentials.
- **Credential–competence gap**: graduates arriving in the workplace with degrees that overstate their actual capability, shifting training cost onto employers.
- **AI-proof assessment**: a format whose evidence of learning cannot be outsourced — real-time articulation, personal reflection, collaborative production.
- **Formative vs summative emphasis**: shifting weight toward continuous progress evidence rather than a single terminal judgment.
- **Uneven playing field**: students with access to better AI tools out-scoring peers who work unaided, compounding existing socioeconomic advantage.
- **De-skilling risk in safety-critical fields**: in healthcare, engineering, and law the competence gap is not an academic abstraction but a liability.

## Mental Models
- **Think of AI as a grade amplifier, not a cheating device.** Much AI use is not deliberate dishonesty — students often do not see it as unethical, especially when institutions have not drawn the line between Grammarly and a generative model. Ambiguity, not malice, drives most of the volume.
- **Use "can this be defended live?" as the design test.** If a student could not explain and justify the submission in a five-minute conversation, the assessment is not measuring them.
- **Treat detection as a lagging indicator, never a control.** Detection tools trail the models permanently; designing assessments that do not require detection is the only durable move.
- **Use the reason students reach for AI to diagnose the assessment.** Rote tasks, perceived irrelevance, and disengagement predict AI substitution. If a task invites outsourcing, that is information about the task.

## Worked Example
Converting a standard essay-plus-exam module to an AI-resistant design, applying the matrix and the incremental principle together:

1. **Replace the terminal essay with a semester-spanning project** anchored in module-specific context — a real problem, real data, or the student's own placement/professional setting. This satisfies the authenticity criterion: the AI has no access to the student's specific situation.
2. **Break the project into staged, individually reviewed deliverables** — proposal, outline, annotated progress report, draft, final. Each is marked for development, not polish. The instructor now has a trajectory to compare against, so a sudden discontinuity in capability is visible without any detector.
3. **Change the output format from prose to multimedia or presentation** — a video, poster, or site. This moves the assessment from Understand/Evaluate to Create/Apply on Bloom's, and multimodal production remains hard for text models to substitute.
4. **Add an oral defence or viva component** in which the student presents multiple candidate solutions and justifies the one chosen. Record the sessions for moderation and mark against a structured rubric so consistency and bias are controlled. This is the load-bearing element: it assesses real-time understanding, and it cannot be delegated.
5. **Add peer review with reasoned justification** — students critique each other's staged work and must explain the reasoning behind each evaluative judgment, not just deliver a verdict. Generic AI-written feedback is conspicuous here.
6. **Close with a reflective component** requiring personal examples and self-assessment of the learning journey, including how AI was used.
7. **Publish the AI-use rules on the assignment sheet itself**, with concrete acceptable/unacceptable examples, so the ambiguity that drives most misuse is removed before the work starts.

The result assesses collaboration, communication, technical execution, and reasoning — dimensions the original essay never touched — and it is markedly harder to outsource at any single point.

## Key Takeaways
1. Design so detection is unnecessary; do not build an integrity strategy on tools that permanently trail the models.
2. Marginal passes are the real signal: students who now scrape through are the ones who have learned nothing and will never be remediated.
3. Move weight from summative endpoints to formative, staged evidence — a trajectory is far harder to fake than a document.
4. Add one real-time component (viva, defence, Q&A) to every high-weight assessment; it is the single highest-leverage change.
5. Anchor tasks in context AI does not have — this cohort, this placement, this student's experience.
6. Write explicit acceptable/unacceptable AI-use examples into assignment sheets; ambiguity, not malice, drives most misuse.
7. Assessment redesign without policy, faculty training, and digital literacy support will not hold — treat them as one programme.
8. Varied formats also serve equity: students who cannot demonstrate understanding through timed prose get other routes to show it.
